## Design Context

### Users
Anyone who clicks a game on greykit.com. **Most have never seen the 1997 Parker Brothers box
and do not know what Lionheart is.** They arrive curious, on a desktop or a phone, with no rules
in their head and no patience for a rulebook. The interface has to teach itself: a rule should
be explained at the moment it matters, not deferred to a help modal — and not front-loaded into
a modal walkthrough either. One line at a time, under the board, when it becomes true.

Someone who owns Lionheart is a welcome second audience and will judge the port on fidelity —
but the game is designed first for a person meeting it cold.

### Brand Personality
Dry, practitioner, unhurried — the greykit house voice. Never zany, never emoji. The field
report reads like a report: what happened, to whom, on which square. Three words: **terse,
exact, wry.**

Emotional goal: a small tactical wargame you can learn in two minutes and lose in ten. The
tension belongs to the dice and the panic cascade, never to the chrome.

### Aesthetic Direction
- **Tactical tribe**, shared with PumaRally, PumaCity, PumaTower and PumaTycoon. Moved here from
  Cybersec-CRT on 2026-10-02. Deep navy ground `#05070f`, cyan `#54f2ff` for the live thing,
  red `#ff5266` only for harm; monospace; no scanline, no vignette. The armies keep their names
  and take the tribe's own hues: Gold is its amber `#ffb000`, Green is its green `#4ade6a`. The
  first `:root` block is PumaRally's TOKENS table, byte-identical to PumaCity's retyping of it.
  Design *within* it — this is house style, not a default reached for because dark looks cool.
- **The game is a frame set on a field, after PumaCity.** The frame is centred in the window
  both ways; behind it, a line-drawn field at dusk: a keep on the far hill, a pine ridge,
  and the two camps in front, Gold's pavilions on the left and Green's on the right. Their
  pennants are the only game hues the scenery borrows. It sits in the side margins and the
  bottom band, stays low contrast, never moves, and never competes with the board. On a
  phone it drops away and the frame comes off: the board needs every pixel.
- **The frame holds still.** One width in every phase and the board one size, set by the
  window alone, because over scenery every change of size slides the field in and out
  behind it. The battle plan rides in the rail rather than under the board for the same
  reason. The space around the frame is the scenery's to fill — never pad the frame to a
  fixed height; a hint under the board may grow it, and that is the only thing that may.
- **Structural reference: *The Crescent Hawk's Inception* (1988).** Flat boxed panels with
  notched small-caps labels and chunky pixel portraits. Framed and square, never rounded,
  never shadowed.
- **The mouse is the primary interface.** Play happens by clicking the board: a base, then a
  square to move to or a ring to attack, with two curved arrows in a selected base's front corners to
  turn it. The keyboard mirrors all of it, but it is documented in the help rather than displayed as
  a bar of shortcuts. On a coarse pointer those corner arrows come off the base entirely — two
  44px targets do not fit on a 28px base — and become a pair of controls under the board.
- **Motion: modern polish under the retro skin.** Contemporary craft — staggered reveals, real
  hover feedback, exponential easing — wearing the tribe's colours. Not period restraint, and not
  glitch pastiche.
- **No invented fiction.** The unit names are the printed ones, and the armies keep the
  rulebook's Gold; side 0 is called Green rather than the printed Silver because the palette
  renders it in green, and a side named for a colour it plainly is not costs more than the
  fidelity is worth. An earlier draft dressed the game in an
  anthropomorphic puma people; it was flavour the port did not need, and pumas are solitary
  anyway. The name PumaHeart is the portfolio's, not the game's.
- **Dark only.** No web fonts, no external assets, nothing fetched at runtime.
- **Colour-blind accommodation is explicitly not a priority**, matching PumaTriage's stance:
  the source game distinguishes its armies by colour too. Where a second channel is nearly free
  — the shaved base shape, the figure pips — keep it. Do not spend design budget defending it.

### Design Principles
1. **Faithful before flavoured.** A Lionheart player must never re-learn a rule because of the
   theme. Where the 1997 rules are silent, say so in the open rather than inventing quietly.
2. **Teach in place.** The player has never seen the box. Motion, labels and layout should
   explain a rule at the moment it becomes relevant.
3. **Tribe identity is fixed, so spend the effort on hierarchy, rhythm and motion** — not on
   palette or type. Cyan marks the one thing to look at, amber and green are the two armies, red
   is only harm.
4. **The figure count is the game.** It is the unit's toughness and its dice pool at once. Draw
   it; never merely imply it.
5. **Motion earns its place by explaining something.** Every animation teaches a rule, confirms
   an action, or shows where something went. Polish is welcome; restlessness is not.
